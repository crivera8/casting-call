A field guide adapted from doing-gender research

Casting CallAssessing how an AI gets gendered

By Vanessa Conzon & Allie Feldberg

This AI tool is designed to help management researchers reflect on the gender-typing of the AI tools they use. It also offers a feature to help one take a gender-conscious approach to designing one's own AI tools.

Every AI assistant has a director who chose its name, its voice, and its manner — whether they meant to or not. This walks through what those choices signal, and what the research says it might mean when people interact with them again and again.

Before the curtain rises

You'll need to be signed in to Claude to use this tool.

Claude built this, and Claude makes mistakes. Guessing a name's gender connotation across languages and cultures, describing a voice or an image it hasn't actually seen or heard, and finding out who really designed something — all of that is genuinely hard, and often the information just isn't public. Treat everything below as a starting point for your own judgment, not a verdict.

A note on bias: like any AI trained on existing scholarship, Casting Call can gravitate toward familiar methods, frameworks, and academic traditions. If your work sits outside that mainstream — non-Western contexts, understudied populations, less conventional methods — weigh its input accordingly, and don't let it substitute for other perspectives.

Character breakdown sheet

How are these categorized as masculine or feminine?

No, there isn't a lookup table. This tool doesn't check a name against a gender-name database, or an avatar against a shape/color taxonomy — no such database exists here. In manual mode, you make each call, guided by the rules below. In search mode, Claude makes the same call using its general knowledge and whatever the web search turns up, described in a short note; a simple keyword check then tries to translate that note into a dropdown selection, which is imperfect — always review it before revealing the placement. Either way, the decision is a judgment call applied consistently, not a database hit.

Name: is it a name conventionally given to one gender in the relevant language or cultural context — the way "Michael" reads masculine and "Emma" reads feminine in most English-speaking contexts? This is convention, not universal truth: naming patterns vary by culture, era, and community, and plenty of names are shared across genders. Treat it as a starting point you're free to override.

Pronouns: the most direct of the four signals — whatever pronoun the product is actually referred to by, officially or in common usage. No inference required, just reporting.

Voice: coded primarily by pitch, since listeners reliably use it as a cue to categorize a voice as male- or female-sounding, and default voice choices in AI products have been shown to trigger the same gendered responses human voices do (Nass, Moon, & Green, 1997; Costa, 2018; Mooshammer & Etzrodt, 2022). Lower-pitched defaults read masculine-coded; higher-pitched defaults read feminine-coded.

Imagery/avatar: the hardest call of the four, and the one most worth double-checking. For a humanlike avatar, this follows the same body-shape and styling cues people use to read a person as male- or female-presenting (Borau, 2025). For a non-human avatar — an abstract shape, an object, a color scheme — there's no formal taxonomy behind the call; it draws on general cultural association instead: angular or hard-edged shapes, cool colors, and tool/vehicle/machinery imagery tend to read as masculine-coded, while soft or rounded shapes, warm or pastel colors, and floral/organic imagery tend to read as feminine-coded. These associations are genuinely contestable and culturally specific, so note your reasoning in the notes field rather than leaving it implicit.

Job/task: coded using research on which occupations and job functions are stereotypically associated with each gender at work — admin, care, and support work reads as feminine-coded; technical, quantitative, and strategic work reads as masculine-coded (Singh, Chayko, Inamdar, & Floegel, 2020; Eagly & Karau, 2002).

Tone/manner: coded using the standard distinction between agentic traits — assertive, direct, competitive, read as masculine — and communal traits — warm, deferential, accommodating, read as feminine (Rudman & Glick, 2001; Charlesworth & Banaji, 2022; Manzi, Caleo, & Heilman, 2024).

None of this is a hard rule or a validated instrument — it's the convention this tool applies consistently across every field, grounded in the same research the underlying paper draws on, so "masculine-coded" and "feminine-coded" mean roughly the same thing everywhere they appear here. sets out how strong the evidence behind each of these six calls actually is, how the reading list was assembled, and why your own judgment is part of what gets recorded here.

The goal here is specific: help you build something that doesn't reaffirm the gender stereotype tied to its job — not just describe your odds either way. Using Conzon & Feldberg's model, you'll make the same four calls a real director makes for an AI actor, deliberately, in the order design actually happens: lock in the job first, then decide how it presents.

3

What this design predicts

Why bother with any of this?

Gender is one of the very first things people use to sort the world (Ridgeway, 2011) — it takes almost nothing, a name or a pitch of voice, for someone to place a thing as "male" or "female" and start expecting certain behavior from it (Martin & Slepian, 2020; Nass, Moon, & Green, 1997). That happens with people. Research on interactive AI suggests it happens with AI too (Fortunati et al., 2022; Martin & Mason, 2023; Han et al., 2025), and increasingly, AI assistants sit in the middle of everyday work: booking travel, running numbers, answering customer questions.

The concern isn't only that an AI might reflect bias buried in its training data — the more familiar "algorithmic bias" story. It's that an AI with a name, a voice, and a manner is performing gender the way a coworker might, in every single interaction (West & Zimmerman, 1987). A scheduling assistant with a feminine name and a deferential tone, used thousands of times a day across a company, quietly rehearses the idea that supportive, subordinate work is women's work (Ridgeway & Correll, 2004) — and repeated exposure of this kind, at scale, has been shown to shift the gender associations people carry (Guilbeault et al., 2024). The reverse pairing — an assistant with the same markers doing confident, technical work without softening for pushback — can quietly work against that idea instead.

That second possibility is really the point: these are design choices, not laws of nature. Someone decided the name, the voice, whether it apologizes when you're short with it. A few real teams have already made that choice deliberately — Sage built its accounting chatbot "Pegg" to read as gender-neutral (Sherriff, 2018), and a coalition of technologists built "Q," a voice designed not to register as clearly male or female (Carpenter, 2019; Mortada, 2019). Being able to name these choices is a first step toward making them on purpose rather than by default.

What it's actually good for

  • It makes a default into a decision. Most of these choices were never argued for. Naming the ones that were made by inertia is most of the value, and it's available whether or not you accept anything the model predicts.
  • It applies the same convention every time. The conventions are contestable, but they're applied consistently across every field and every product, so two assessments can be compared to each other even if you'd have drawn the lines somewhere else.
  • It keeps markers and behavior apart. Blending them into a single "how gendered is it" number would hide the one configuration that matters most — markers pointing one way while the work points the other. That pairing is only visible if the two are scored separately.
  • It shows its work. Every call comes with the reasoning and the sources behind it, so you can disagree with one specific coding decision rather than having to accept or reject the whole verdict.
  • In design mode, it front-loads a trade-off. The cost of shipping a deliberately misaligned, non-adaptive agent is real, and it's much cheaper to weigh before launch than after.

What this doesn't tell you

  • It's binary by design, because the field mostly still is. This framework sorts things into "masculine" and "feminine," which flattens real people and, arguably, AI too (Ridgeway & Saperstein, 2024). A handful of real designers have tried to build deliberately genderless assistants — that possibility sits outside what this tool can score.
  • Gender isn't the only axis worth checking. Names, voices, and imagery can just as easily be racialized, aged, classed, or accented — a name can read as gendered and read as tied to a particular ethnicity at the same time, and this tool has nothing to say about that.
  • The "reinforces vs. weakens" language is a hypothesis, not a finding. It comes from a theoretical model built on hypothetical illustrations of how repeated exposure to an AI actor might shift the stereotypes someone holds (Conzon & Feldberg, working paper) — not from a controlled study measuring that it does. Use it to think, not to conclude.
  • One assessment is a snapshot. What the underlying theory actually cares about is repeated exposure over time and how real audiences respond to it — a single pass through a form can't capture that.
  • In search mode it reads the marketing, not the product. One web search, mostly over material the company wrote about itself, with no access to the actual voice or interface. A gap between how something is described and how it behaves is exactly the kind of thing this can't see.
  • One coder is one coder. A score produced by a single person in a single sitting records that person's reading. It is a data point, not a property of the product (O'Connor & Joffe, 2020).

How this tool worksProvenance, evidence, and limits

Everything behind the readings the tool gives you: where the information comes from, how strong the evidence is behind each individual call, how anyone measures stereotype content in the first place, how this reading list was assembled, and what the tool is doing with your own judgment.

Where all of this comes from

A tool that hands you a reading of something as loaded as gender should be willing to say where that reading came from. This section is that account — not just which papers are cited, but how the information gets in, how the reading list was assembled, and which calls rest on solid evidence versus a judgment someone could reasonably contest. The practice of shipping a system with a plain-language account of what went into it, what it's for, and where it breaks comes out of the AI documentation literature (Mitchell et al., 2019; Gebru et al., 2021).

1. How the information about a product gets here

There are exactly two routes in, and they behave differently.

If you fill in the sheet yourself

Every fact comes from you. The tool contributes no information about the product whatsoever — it only applies the coding conventions below to what you typed and does the arithmetic. If you're wrong about the product, the output is wrong, and nothing here will catch it.

If you ask Claude to look it up

What actually happens: the tool sends your query to Anthropic's API with instructions to run at most one web search, then return a fixed set of structured codes, a short reason for each, and links. That's a quick scan of public, web-indexed material — product pages, marketing copy, press coverage, documentation — not a literature review. One search is a deliberate trade-off for speed, and it means a hard-to-find fact will usually come back as "not sure" rather than being chased down.

It has never seen or heard the product. Every voice and imagery call in search mode is inferred from text describing a voice or an avatar, not from listening or looking. That's a real gap between what's being coded and what an actual audience encounters.

Check the links before you trust them. Language models fabricate citations at rates that matter: in one study of 636 references generated across 84 literature reviews, 55% of GPT-3.5's and 18% of GPT-4's were fabricated outright, and among the real ones, 43% and 24% respectively carried substantive errors (Walters & Wilder, 2023). This tool searches first and is instructed never to invent a URL, which helps, but it doesn't make the failure mode go away.

The pool it draws from is itself skewed. A web search returns what is indexed, English-language, recent, and search-optimized — heavily weighted toward marketing material written by the same people who chose the gender markers. And the underlying material carries its own bias: occupational imagery online is already gender-skewed relative to reality (Singh et al., 2020), and image corpora amplify gender bias more than text does (Guilbeault et al., 2024). "What the internet says about this product" inherits all of that.

It can come back partial. If the response runs long it gets truncated, and the tool salvages whatever complete fields it can rather than failing outright. When that happens you'll see a note saying so — treat those results as a first draft.

3. How anyone knows what the stereotype is

The step above says which sources sit behind each call. This one is the question underneath it: when this tool asserts that warmth reads feminine or that technical work reads masculine, where did that content come from? It isn't intuition, and it isn't the authors' opinion. Stereotype content is something researchers measure, by four broad methods.

Asking large representative samples, over decades

The oldest method is simply asking people whether a trait is more true of women, more true of men, or equally true of both. Pooling 16 nationally representative U.S. polls covering roughly 30,000 adults between 1946 and 2018, women's perceived advantage in communal traits — affectionate, emotional, compassionate — actually grew over that period, while men's perceived advantage in agentic traits held steady, and belief in equal competence rose sharply (Eagly, Nater, Miller, Kaufmann, & Sczesny, 2020).

Two things follow for this tool. First, the agentic-versus-communal split it uses to code tone is a measured, repeatedly replicated pattern rather than a convenient dichotomy. Second, that measurement has a date and a country attached to it, and the content moved within the window studied. A coding convention drawn from it is a snapshot of a population, not a fact about men and women.

Measuring associations people don't report on a survey

Self-report captures what people are willing and able to say. Reaction-time measures capture associations that show up whether or not someone endorses them, and large-scale collection through platforms like Project Implicit has made it possible to track those associations over time. Across a decade of continuously collected tests, both implicit and explicit gender stereotypes linking men to science and career and women to arts and family have measurably weakened, though from a strong baseline and at a slow rate (Charlesworth & Banaji, 2022).

The consequence for anyone using this tool: "I don't personally think admin work is women's work" is not evidence that the association isn't operating. That gap between endorsed belief and measured association is much of why a tool like this asks you to write your reading down instead of trusting your sense of your own neutrality.

Reading it off the culture's own text and images

A third approach measures associations in large bodies of language and imagery rather than in people. Word embeddings trained on ordinary text reproduce the same associations the reaction-time measures find in humans, including career-versus-family gender associations (Caliskan, Bryson, & Narayanan, 2017). Applied across a century of text and checked against census data, the same technique tracks occupational gender associations shifting in step with who actually held those jobs (Garg, Schiebinger, Jurafsky, & Zou, 2018). Gender stereotypes turn up in corpora of children's language as consistently as in adults' (Charlesworth, Yang, Mann, Kurdi, & Banaji, 2021), and images carry gender bias more strongly than text does (Guilbeault et al., 2024).

This is the method closest to what this tool is actually about, because an AI product's name, voice, and avatar are exactly the kind of cultural artifact these studies measure. It's also the method that makes the "audience" half of the framework tractable at all.

Where the content comes from in the first place

Measuring a stereotype doesn't explain why it holds the content it does. The leading account is that stereotype content is inferred from the roles people observe a group occupying: watch a group concentrated in caretaking work and you come to believe that group is caring by disposition. When people are given information about a group's typical roles, the stereotypes they form follow that role information — including when the roles described are hypothetical or set in the future (Koenig & Eagly, 2014). Perceived mismatch between a gender role and a job role is then what produces penalties for the person who doesn't fit (Eagly & Karau, 2002).

This is the piece that makes an AI assistant worth assessing at all. If stereotype content tracks the distribution of who is observed doing what, then filling workplaces with feminine-marked agents doing support work is not merely reflecting an existing stereotype. It is adding observations to the pile the next inference gets drawn from. That's the mechanism the whole framework rests on, and it's why "it's just a voice option" and "it's just a name" don't dispose of the question.

What this means for reading your own results. The stereotype content encoded in these conventions is measured, mostly in the United States, mostly in English, mostly in recent decades — and it demonstrably moves. So a coding decision is anchored to a time and place, and the honest way to use it is to record why you coded something the way you did, not just what you coded. The reasoning survives a shift in the convention. The code alone doesn't. Note also that this tool runs none of these measurements: it applies conventions distilled from that literature, which is a real step removed from the evidence.

4. How the reading list itself was put together

How the reference list was chosen — and what it isn't

This is a selected bibliography, not a systematic review. There was no search protocol, no pre-registered inclusion and exclusion criteria, no screening of everything published on the topic. Saying so matters, because a list of twenty citations can look like a survey of a field when it's a curated starting point.

The sources arrived by three routes. Most come from the bibliography of the underlying working paper — that's where the framework itself and the core gender-theory citations originate, and it's the authoritative layer here. A second set came from targeted follow-up searches for the specific empirical claims this tool makes on its own, mainly the coding conventions above: voice pitch, occupational stereotyping, avatar cues, agentic-versus-communal traits. A third, small set are journalistic sources for particular product facts — the Pegg and Q examples — where no academic account exists.

Peer-reviewed work was preferred but not required, and the list skews toward English-language research in North American and European contexts, which is a real limit on a framework about cultural convention. Where a claim rests on a single study, that's a thin foundation, and the badges above are the honest signal of which ones those are. Nothing was left out for cutting against the framework.

Were these articles actually read?

Not in full, and the honest answer differs by layer. This matters enough to state plainly, because "it has a citation" and "someone checked the claim against the paper" are different guarantees, and a reference list looks identical either way.

Sources inherited from the working paper's bibliography are cited here as that paper cites them. The articles were not independently read for this tool, and the claims attached to them rest on the authors' reading rather than a fresh one. For the gender-theory foundations that is a reasonable place to stand, since it is the authors' own field and their own paper. It does mean an error there would propagate into this tool unchanged.

Sources added specifically for this tool — the documentation, stereotype-measurement, citation-accuracy, and coding-reliability literature — were checked against abstracts, publisher records, and indexing databases to confirm authors, journal, year, volume, and pages, and to confirm that the specific claim made here matches what the abstract reports. Full texts were not read.

What that level of checking catches, and what it misses. It catches wrong volume numbers, invented references, and misattributions — one citation in an earlier version of this tool was corrected precisely this way, when the abstract showed the paper covered six attitude domains that did not include gender. It does not catch a finding that an abstract states cleanly but the paper itself qualifies, a result that failed to replicate later, or a methodological problem visible only in the full text. If you are going to rely on any specific claim here for your own work, read the paper.

5. One of the things being assessed here is you

Worth being direct about, because it isn't obvious from the interface: when you fill in this sheet by hand, the tool is asking you to decide whether a name, a voice, or a picture reads as masculine or feminine. There's no lookup table behind that. So what comes out reflects your associations as much as it reflects any property of the product.

That's the design, not a flaw in it. The moment you decide that an angular blue logo "reads masculine," a tacit association has become explicit and arguable — which is the only state in which it can be examined. And the reading is happening anyway: people assign gender to agents carrying no gender markers at all, defaulting to masculine when cues are absent (Han et al., 2025), and context shifts how an ambiguous voice gets heard (Mooshammer & Etzrodt, 2022). The audience is doing this whether or not anyone writes it down. This just writes it down.

So if you find yourself confident about a call you can't actually justify, that's worth sitting with. It's information about the stereotype you're carrying, not a bug in the form.

The practical version: have someone else code the same product independently, then compare, and pay attention to where you disagree rather than resolving it quickly. Comparing independent coders is standard practice in qualitative analysis precisely because it surfaces which categories are ambiguous and forces the criteria to be stated out loud (O'Connor & Joffe, 2020). Disagreement isn't noise here. It's the most interesting thing the tool produces.
