A field guide adapted from doing-gender research

Casting CallAssessing how an AI gets gendered

By Vanessa Conzon & Allie Feldberg

Every AI assistant has a director who chose its name, its voice, and its manner — whether they meant to or not. This walks through what those choices signal, and what the research says it might mean when people interact with them again and again.

Before the curtain rises

Claude built this, and Claude makes mistakes. Guessing a name's gender connotation across languages and cultures, describing a voice or an image it hasn't actually seen or heard, and finding out who really designed something — all of that is genuinely hard, and often the information just isn't public. Treat everything below as a starting point for your own judgment, not a verdict.

Design studio

The goal here is specific: help you build something that doesn't reaffirm the gender stereotype tied to its job — not just describe your odds either way. Using Conzon & Feldberg's model, you'll make the same four calls a real director makes for an AI actor, deliberately, in the order design actually happens: lock in the job first, then decide how it presents.

Be honest about the stereotype, even if you don't like it — you can't avoid reaffirming a bias you haven't named.
B The manner

D How much it bends
If your goal is to not reaffirm the stereotype, pick nonadaptive. Per the model, that's the only one of the four pathways that loosens a stereotype rather than reaffirming it — and only when it's paired with markers that already don't match the job. Even a deliberate mismatch will drift back into reaffirming the stereotype if it's adaptive, because it'll fold toward "expected" behavior the first time someone reacts badly to it. This is also a real product trade-off: some users read nonadaptive as unhelpful or stubborn. That cost is worth weighing on purpose, not a reason to default back to adaptive.
E You, the director
Per the model, three things constrain a director's choices whether they notice or not: your own gender attitudes, your authority to make a less "expected" design stick even under pushback, and the training data or examples you're limited to. Naming these honestly is most of the work.

What this design predicts

Why bother with any of this?

Gender is one of the very first things people use to sort the world (Ridgeway, 2011) — it takes almost nothing, a name or a pitch of voice, for someone to place a thing as "male" or "female" and start expecting certain behavior from it (Martin & Slepian, 2020; Nass, Moon, & Green, 1997). That happens with people. Research on interactive AI suggests it happens with AI too (Fortunati et al., 2022; Martin & Mason, 2023; Han et al., 2025), and increasingly, AI assistants sit in the middle of everyday work: booking travel, running numbers, answering customer questions.

The concern isn't only that an AI might reflect bias buried in its training data — the more familiar "algorithmic bias" story. It's that an AI with a name, a voice, and a manner is performing gender the way a coworker might, in every single interaction (West & Zimmerman, 1987). A scheduling assistant with a feminine name and a deferential tone, used thousands of times a day across a company, quietly rehearses the idea that supportive, subordinate work is women's work (Ridgeway & Correll, 2004) — and repeated exposure of this kind, at scale, has been shown to shift the gender associations people carry (Guilbeault et al., 2024). The reverse pairing — an assistant with the same markers doing confident, technical work without softening for pushback — can quietly work against that idea instead.

That second possibility is really the point: these are design choices, not laws of nature. Someone decided the name, the voice, whether it apologizes when you're short with it. A few real teams have already made that choice deliberately — Sage built its accounting chatbot "Pegg" to read as gender-neutral (Sherriff, 2018), and a coalition of technologists built "Q," a voice designed not to register as clearly male or female (Carpenter, 2019; Mortada, 2019). Being able to name these choices is a first step toward making them on purpose rather than by default.

What this doesn't tell you

  • It's binary by design, because the field mostly still is. This framework sorts things into "masculine" and "feminine," which flattens real people and, arguably, AI too (Ridgeway & Saperstein, 2024). A handful of real designers have tried to build deliberately genderless assistants — that possibility sits outside what this tool can score.
  • Gender isn't the only axis worth checking. Names, voices, and imagery can just as easily be racialized, aged, classed, or accented — a name can read as gendered and read as tied to a particular ethnicity at the same time, and this tool has nothing to say about that.
  • The "amplifies vs. attenuates" language is a hypothesis, not a finding. It comes from a theoretical model built on hypothetical illustrations of how repeated exposure to an AI actor might shift the stereotypes someone holds (Conzon & Feldberg, working paper) — not from a controlled study measuring that it does. Use it to think, not to conclude.
  • One assessment is a snapshot. What the underlying theory actually cares about is repeated exposure over time and how real audiences respond to it — a single pass through a form can't capture that.
